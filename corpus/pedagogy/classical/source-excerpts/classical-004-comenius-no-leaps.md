## Text

Nature, in its formative processes, begins with the universal and ends with the particular.

Nature makes no leaps, but proceeds step by step.

## Context

Two of the nine principle-headings Keatinge's translation gives in Chapter XVI, the chapter that sets out a method of teaching and learning "with certainty" by imitating nature. Each heading in the chapter is followed by an illustration from nature (a bird forming its egg, a tree putting out its growth), a "Deviation" describing how the schools violate the principle, and a "Rectification" applying it to teaching; those paragraphs are not reproduced here because I could not verify their exact wording. The two headings are quoted verbatim: both were confirmed word-for-word by a secondary source reprinting Keatinge's list of the nine principles (Christian History Institute), and they match my recollection of the text. Not verified from this session: that these are the sixth and seventh principles respectively (I believe so), Keatinge's exact chapter title, and page numbers — none claimed. Note for Drew: Chapter XVII repeats several of these headings in a parallel list on teaching with ease, and that chapter's sixth principle reads "Nature does not hurry, but advances slowly"; the two sentences above are the Chapter XVI forms. Hearth's reading: this is the classical tutor's planning rule — whole before parts, easy before hard, no step skipped — and the warrant for the restraint against asking a grammar-stage child to leap to analysis.
