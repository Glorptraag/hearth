## Text

When Comenius turns from general method to the teaching of the sciences — by which he means knowledge of the natural world — he lays down that such knowledge rests first on the senses. How certain a child's knowledge of a thing is depends more on the testimony of their own eyes, ears and hands than on anything else, because a thing makes its mark on the senses directly and on the understanding only by way of the senses. Whatever is understood was first perceived; the mind has nothing to work on until the senses have delivered something to it. From this he draws what he calls a golden rule for teachers: put everything, as far as it can be done, in front of the senses. Let what can be seen be shown to the eyes, what can be heard to the ears, what has a smell to the nose, what can be tasted or handled to tongue and hand — and if a thing can reach several senses at once, let it reach them all. Words about a thing should follow the thing rather than stand in for it, and where the thing itself cannot be had, a picture or a model of it should be.

## Context

Original Hearth paraphrase of the opening sections of Keatinge's Chapter XX, "The method of the sciences, specifically": the argument that knowledge of things begins in sense-perception, the scholastic maxim that nothing is in the understanding that was not first in the senses, the "golden rule" that everything be placed before as many senses as possible, and the allowance for pictures or models where the object itself is unavailable. Search attributed a sentence beginning "The truth and certainty of science depend more on the witness of the senses" to Comenius via scholarly sources, and I recall Keatinge's phrase "golden rule for teachers", but neither the registered archive.org scan nor the Wikisource mirror was reachable from this session and I could not confirm the exact rule text, so this is a paraphrase throughout. Section numbers within Chapter XX not claimed. Hearth's reading: the classical grammar stage is not words-only — Comenius insists the facts a child stores should be met first through the senses, which is why a classical home can log a creek walk or a kitchen experiment as grammar-stage work without any strain on the tradition.
