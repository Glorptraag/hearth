## Trigger

The parent has set out fine work — sandpaper letters, threading, the button frame, the knobbed cylinders, a tray of tweezers and pompoms — and the child will not come to it, or comes and slides off the chair within a minute. He roams, climbs, knocks things over, "never keeps still". The parent reads it as a failed concentration, a sign he is not ready for the method, or defiance.

## Response

Montessori reads the roaming body as work that has not yet been given its material. In the Handbook she is blunt: the small child "if left without guidance, is disorderly in his movements", and this is "the special characteristic of the little child" — he "never keeps still" and "touches everything", and that is what adults call unruliness. But "in these movements the little one is seeking the very exercise which will organize and coordinate the movements useful to man." The adult's "keep still" is "monotonous and useless". The tradition's answer is not to demand stillness but to give "order" to his movements, "leading them to those actions towards which his efforts are actually tending". Once the movement has a direction and an end, "he himself grows quiet and contented, and becomes an active worker".

So the order the tradition expects is large before small. The special gymnastics Montessori thinks necessary between three and six "refer chiefly to walking": the line on the floor walked heel to toe like a tightrope, rising from and sitting down on a chair, carrying objects, moving the light tables that two four-year-olds could lift between them. Practical life is where the big movements live and have a purpose — carrying a full jug of water across the room without spilling, scrubbing a table, sweeping, carrying a tray, polishing, hanging washing, walking the line with a bell that must not ring. Each is whole-body work with a definite end, and the end is what settles the child. In her own listing of the movements to be taught, the sequence runs from rising, sitting, walking and the line, through carrying and setting down objects, to "finally" the complex movements of dressing: the whole body first, the hands last. Fine work offered before that order is in place is not refused out of defiance; it simply cannot hold a body that still needs to move.

Montessori also insists that movement is where the child rests. "True rest for muscles, intended by nature for action, is in orderly action"; a child who moves "in a disorderly, disconnected manner" is under strain, while purposeful movement multiplies his energy and his calm. She tells of a baby in a Roman park filling a pail with gravel whose nurse, kindly, filled it for him and lifted him into his carriage, and was met with screams, because he had wanted the motion of filling, not the full pail. The adult judged the child "by his own measure", thinking the aim was the object. The parent who wants the sandpaper letters done is in the nurse's position; the child wants the movement first.

What a Montessori adult would typically do, then, is begin the morning with the big work rather than with the tray — not as a warm-up or a way to "burn off energy" before the real lesson, but as the real work. Present the movement slowly and silently (how the jug is held, where the cloth lives), then withdraw and watch. Leave the fine material on the shelf, available and unremarked. What she watches for is whether, after twenty minutes of carrying, pouring and walking the line, the child drifts to a tray on his own; whether he seeks heavy things to lift; whether the days that begin with movement end with longer concentration. Over weeks the balance shifts by itself. The quiet of a well-running Montessori room is movement given order, never stillness imposed.

## Anti-pattern

"Sit still and finish, then you can run around" — this inverts the tradition's order and makes movement an external reward, which Montessori rejects alongside stickers and prizes. Reading restlessness as a reading or writing problem and adding more table time. Interrupting a child mid-carry to redirect him to the "proper" lesson. Commanded exercise — star jumps on cue, a gym-class routine — which Montessori dismisses as gymnastics whose "guiding spirit" is coercion. Removing the fine material because he "isn't ready for it", rather than leaving it in reach for the day he is. Treating the roaming as evidence that the method does not suit this child.
