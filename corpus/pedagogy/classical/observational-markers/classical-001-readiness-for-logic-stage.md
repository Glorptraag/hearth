## What it indicates

In the stage reading of the trivium that Dorothy Sayers proposed in 1947 — and that most classical home educators since have adopted — a child's appetite shifts, somewhere around ten to twelve and earlier in some children, from collecting to contesting. The younger child delighted in the words themselves: chants, lists, the names of things, the whole poem by heart. The child at this threshold starts to want reasons, to catch adults in inconsistencies, to argue a point for the pleasure of arguing it, and to insist that rules be fair and applied the same way every time. The tradition reads this not as a behaviour problem but as the mind asking for its next tool: having been furnished with facts and words (the grammar), it now wants to learn what follows from what (the logic, or dialectic). The marker matters because the tutor's response changes with it — chanting alone is no longer enough, and disputation, definition and the Socratic question become the daily work. It is a readiness marker, not an arrival: a child who argues constantly and badly is a child ready to be taught to argue well. It is not yet a sign of readiness for the rhetoric stage, which asks for the further ability to express a reasoned position persuasively and well; opinion without the tools behind it is the thing this stage exists to repair. The marker often appears in one area before another — a child may be arguing about fairness at the park while still happily chanting Latin endings — and the tradition treats that unevenness as normal.

## Look for

- Argues for the sake of it, taking the opposite side of whatever is said and enjoying the exchange rather than being distressed by it
- Spots inconsistencies — between what a parent said yesterday and today, between two books, between a rule and how it was applied — and names them
- Asks "why" and "how do you know" where a year ago the question was "what is it called"
- Wants rules to be fair, and objects to unfairness in principle, not only when it costs them
- Finds memorised material babyish or pointless while still able to recite it perfectly when asked
- Catches an error in the parent's reasoning, or in a book's, and is right
- Can hold two statements side by side and notice they cannot both be true
- Defends a position when challenged instead of abandoning it or simply repeating it more loudly
- Begins to ask what a word actually means in the middle of a disagreement
- Still cannot write their position as well as they can argue it aloud — the expression lags the reasoning, which is expected at this stage
