## What it indicates

When a small child erupts because an adult did up the last button, insists that the cup go in its place and the door be closed by him and no one else, demands that a thing be undone and started again from the beginning, or comes apart when the morning's order is changed — Montessori reads this as the child defending work his development needs right now. "Sensitive periods" is the name she later gave these windows of intense need; in 1912 and 1914 she described the phenomenon without the label. Children "defend themselves with all their might against the adult who would try to help them", and the child "rebels against whatever offends that occult impulse within him which is the voice of nature". The adult who loves him, "fancying that they are helping him", mistakes "his defence of his molested life" for "innate naughtiness".

Two features make the storm legible. Its strength is proportional to the importance of the work: a child does not fight this hard for a thing that does not matter to him. And its satisfaction lies in the act, not the result — "he prefers the action of dressing himself to the state of being dressed". Montessori's later writing treats the insistence on exact order as the same phenomenon seen from another side: the child who must have the same cup in the same spot and the steps in the same sequence is building internal order out of external order, and a disruption outside is felt as a disruption inside.

For the tradition this is a positive marker and a pointer. Positive, because it shows where the child's development is working hardest. A pointer, because whatever he is defending is what the environment should make more available and more unhurried — the extra twenty minutes for dressing is not a delay but the work of the day.

## Look for

- Distress out of proportion to the event when an adult finishes, fixes or hurries a task the child had begun: the last button, the zip, the pouring
- "Me do it", pushing the adult's hand away, or demanding the whole thing be undone so he can do it from the start
- Insistence on exact sequence or placement — the same cup, the same spot, the same order of steps — and upset when it is varied
- The storm resolving quickly once he is allowed to complete the act himself; the completed act, not the outcome, is what satisfies
- The defended task is one he has been repeating lately; this marker and the repetition marker travel together
- Protest that is specific to this task or this step, rather than diffuse resistance to everything (diffuse resistance points elsewhere: tiredness, hunger, too many transitions)
- Slowness in the defended task that is extreme by adult standards but patient and absorbed from the inside
