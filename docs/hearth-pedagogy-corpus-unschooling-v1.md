## Authoring Strategy (How This Corpus Differs From CM)

The Charlotte Mason corpus was buildable end-to-end from public domain primary sources. Mason wrote prolifically, died in 1923, and her complete six-volume series is on Project Gutenberg. I could draft Source Excerpts, Practice Patterns, and Worked Examples in a single working pass with full attribution.

Unschooling is different. Its foundational voices (Holt, Neill, Illich, Gatto) are all in active copyright with no permissive licensing. Its operational substance lives almost entirely in contemporary practitioner output — books, podcasts, blogs, coaching practices — produced by people who are still building their own businesses around that content. **The honest path to a strong Unschooling corpus is to commission a contemporary practitioner to write the operational layers directly,** not to reconstruct them from dated primary texts.

This v1 establishes:

1. **The structural template** — every layer present, formatted identically to CM, ready to receive commissioned content
2. **The evidence layer populated from Gray & Riley CC BY 3.0 research** — the only substantial body of openly licensed material on unschooling outcomes, integrated directly with attribution
3. **A small set of Source Excerpts paraphrased from foundational thinkers** — Holt, Neill, Illich ideas in Hearth's own words with citation, no direct quotation
4. **Three Practice Pattern templates** — fully drafted as examples of what the commissioned author would produce, addressing the highest-frequency triggers from research
5. **Two Worked Examples** drafted as templates including one Australian regulatory scenario (Sue Elvis territory)
6. **Contraindications drawn from documented internal movement tensions** — radical vs relaxed, screen debates, intervention on learning differences, the privilege critique

When the commissioned author engagement closes, this v1 becomes the structural and tonal scaffold. The author fills out Source Excerpts to ~50, Practice Patterns to ~15, Worked Examples to ~20, and the corpus reaches parity with the CM build.

**Recommended commissioning sequence** (from the research, summarised in the licensing needs v2):

- **Pam Laricchia** (livingjoyfully.ca) — primary candidate. Five books, 300+ podcast episodes, already produces Hearth-shaped structured content commercially.
- **Sue Patterson** (unschoolingmom2mom.com) — strong alternative or complement. Coaching practice, 21 published guides, $75/half-hour rate provides pricing anchor.
- **Sue Elvis** (storiesofanunschoolingfamily.com) — Australian regulatory specialist. 27 years successful NSW registration. Co-hosting podcast with Sandra Dodd through 2025. Should author the Australian-context Worked Examples regardless of who handles the international layers.
- **Freya Dawson** (freyadawson.com.au) — secondary Australian voice. Former law lecturer, runs commercial unschooling courses, deepest publicly available NSW registration writing.

### PP-US-001 — Child wants to play video games for hours every day

**trigger_context:** Parent is logging concern about a child (any age, but most commonly 7–14) spending what feels like excessive time on video games. The parent is anxious that this is "not learning" and feels social pressure from family or friends to impose limits.

**tradition_response:**

The unschooling response to gaming runs through three distinct ideas the parent needs to hear in order. The order matters because parents who hear only the third one without the first two often feel the answer has bypassed their actual concern.

First: in unschooling thought, video games are legitimate learning environments. They contain reading (often substantial, often technical), problem-solving, spatial reasoning, planning, resource management, social negotiation, persistence through failure, and frequently a community of practice. The parent who has never engaged with the specific game their child is playing is in a poor position to assess what it contains. The first move is not to limit but to learn — sit beside the child for an hour, ask what they're doing, watch how they think.

Second: when parents have previously restricted games, removal of those restrictions almost always produces a period of compensatory gaming. This looks like addiction, but in the unschooling frame it is more usefully understood as hoarding — the response of any human to a previously-rationed resource suddenly becoming abundant. The hoarding period typically resolves itself over weeks or months as the child learns the resource will not be taken away. Restricting gaming during this period prolongs the hoarding indefinitely; trusting through it lets it complete.

Third: the parent's discomfort with gaming is often less about the gaming and more about a fear that the child is opting out of "real life." The unschooling response is to ensure the rest of life is genuinely available and genuinely interesting — not as a covert intervention to lure the child off the screen, but because a thin offline life makes the screen the only place where anything is happening. Strewing other interesting things, going to interesting places, eating real meals together, making the home a place worth being in — all of this is the parent's actual job here. None of it is enforcement.

A note specific to families with relatives or in-laws who are critical: the parent's task is also to absorb that pressure rather than transmit it to the child. The child should not pay the cost of grandma's discomfort.

**anti_pattern:**

Do not impose time limits as a unilateral parental decision. Do not require "earning" screen time through chores or schoolwork. Do not characterise gaming to the child as a problem to be managed. Do not, when the child is mid-game, deliver lectures about the importance of other activities. All of these reinforce the dynamic the unschooling approach is trying to dissolve.

**grounded_in:** SE-US-002 (parent anxiety as primary challenge), SE-US-007 (strewing), and the documented contemporary unschooling consensus on gaming as articulated by Sandra Dodd, Joyce Fetteroll, Pam Laricchia, and Sue Patterson across their published work.

**situational_triggers:**
- [child_gaming_hours, parent_worried_about_screens, in_laws_disapproving_of_screens, transition_after_school_left, recently_lifted_screen_limits]

**v1_note:** This pattern is drafted as a template demonstrating tone and structure. The commissioned author should refine this with their own voice, examples from their own family, and any nuance specific to age ranges.

### PP-US-002 — Child is not reading yet at age 8

**trigger_context:** Parent is logging that their child, age 7–10, is not yet reading independently. The parent has been trusting the unschooling approach but is starting to feel conventional pressure — from grandparents, from comparison with school-attending friends, from registration paperwork, from their own internalised anxiety.

**tradition_response:**

The unschooling response to late reading is grounded in evidence the parent should know exists. Peter Gray's research on unschooled families found children learning to read on their own anywhere from age 4 to age 11 or later, with an average of around eight and a half. By age 15, the late readers were indistinguishable from the early readers — not just in capacity but in their relationship with reading itself. Many of the late readers became enthusiastic adult readers. Some early readers did not. The age at which a child cracks the code is not predictive of much beyond itself.

This evidence is the first thing the parent needs, because the cultural insistence that a child must be reading by six or seven is so loud and so universal that practitioners need data to brace against it. The data does not say "don't worry" — it says "the worry is responding to a problem that doesn't exist in the way you think it does."

The second thing: in an unschooling household, the genuine non-reader is rare. What is common is the child who can read perfectly well but does not yet do so for their own purposes. They are read to. They listen to audiobooks. They watch videos with captions. They navigate games and websites by recognising shapes of words. They are deeply embedded in literacy without performing the act of reading on demand. The transition from this state to fluent independent reading typically happens suddenly, often within a few weeks, and almost always when the child encounters a specific thing they want to read on their own — a particular book, a game guide, instructions for something they want to make.

The third thing — and this is where Australian families need specific guidance — is how to document this for HEU or NESA without lying and without panicking. The honest description is "child is acquiring literacy through extensive read-aloud, audiobook engagement, environmental print, and digital media engagement; has demonstrated phonemic awareness and word recognition in [these specific contexts]; is on a developmental trajectory documented in research as falling within the normal range for self-directed literacy acquisition." That is a true statement, an accurate statement, and a regulatorily acceptable statement.

The fourth thing — and this matters — is that some children do have a genuine reading difficulty (dyslexia is the most common). Unschooling does not require the parent to ignore this. A child who is showing signs of struggle beyond developmental variation can still benefit from specialist support without that support contradicting the unschooling approach. The honest position within the movement is that this is genuinely contested, and the parent's job is to know their own child. See the Contraindications layer for more on this.

**anti_pattern:**

Do not introduce phonics workbooks or formal reading lessons as a "just in case" — this signals to the child that the parent does not actually trust the process. Do not compare the child to schooled peers. Do not bring up the reading gap in front of the child. Do not panic in front of registration paperwork — write the documentation calmly.

**grounded_in:** SE-US-003 (range of reading ages and indistinguishability by 15), SE-US-002 (parent anxiety), and contemporary practitioner consensus on late reading as a non-problem within the unschooling frame.

**situational_triggers:**
- [child_not_reading, late_reader_concern, HEU_report_literacy, comparing_to_school_peers, grandparent_pressure, parent_self_doubt_on_reading]

### PP-US-003 — Documenting unschooling for state registration (Australian context)

**trigger_context:** Australian unschooling parent is preparing materials for HEU (QLD), NESA (NSW), or VRQA (VIC) registration or annual report. They are anxious because their child's learning does not look like school, the registration paperwork asks for things that look like school, and they don't know how to bridge the gap honestly.

**tradition_response:**

This is the single most consequential operational skill an Australian unschooling parent develops. The good news: it is a learnable skill and many families have done it successfully across all state jurisdictions.

The core move is what Sue Elvis calls **translation** — taking what actually happened (a child built a Minecraft city; a child cared for a sick chicken; a child read four chapters of a Roald Dahl book to their younger sibling; a family went to the museum and the child spent forty minutes at the bug exhibit) and rendering it in language the registration body recognises (computational thinking and spatial design; biology and animal care; reading fluency and prosocial behaviour; informal science learning and sustained attention). The events are real. The translation is the skill.

A few principles make this easier:

- **Document continuously rather than retrospectively.** A short note in the moment ("Liam spent the morning with the chickens, noticed one was lethargic, asked questions about why, looked up symptoms with me on the phone") is much easier to translate than a memory three months old.
- **Use the curriculum framework's own language without lying about what happened.** Australian Curriculum learning area descriptors are general enough that authentic learning almost always maps to several of them. The translation is not invention — it is selection.
- **Photograph and screenshot.** A photo of the child's drawing, the page they read, the screen they were on, the place they visited. These become evidence without requiring narrative construction.
- **Curate, don't fabricate.** Pick the strongest examples from a term, not every moment. A registration body wants to see learning happened — it does not need an exhaustive log.
- **Lead with the child's voice where possible.** Quoting the child ("Mum, why do bees only sting once?") is often more compelling than parental description and is unambiguously authentic.
- **In QLD specifically:** the HEU has reviewed and modernised processes since 2024. The Queensland Home Education unit accepts narrative-based reporting and has a track record of accepting unschooling-style learning plans when they are clearly articulated in curriculum terms.
- **In NSW specifically:** the Authorised Person home visit is the major variable. Documentation should be ready to walk through verbally, not just on paper. Sue Elvis has 27 years of successful NSW visits and has written extensively on what to expect.

The deeper point: the registration system is the parent's problem to solve, not the child's. The child should not be coached, prepped, or asked to perform for the AP visit. The child should continue their day. The parent absorbs the regulatory burden so the unschooling can remain authentic.

**anti_pattern:**

Do not invent learning that did not happen. Do not retroactively add "learning objectives" the child was not pursuing. Do not coach the child to recite things for the AP. Do not let the registration cycle drive the home — the cycle should be a quarterly inconvenience, not a year-round shadow curriculum.

**grounded_in:** Sue Elvis's published work on NSW registration with unschoolers; BarefootChild's QLD learning plan examples; Freya Dawson's published analysis of NSW homeschool registration law.

**situational_triggers:**
- [registration_paperwork_due, AP_visit_upcoming, HEU_report_due, parent_anxious_about_documentation, first_year_registration]

**v1_note:** This pattern should be expanded by Sue Elvis directly. She is the named candidate to author Australian-regulatory entries and has the deepest experience translating unschooled learning into compliant documentation.

## Layer 3 — Observational Markers

Six structured observational markers are the Wave 5 target. The unschooling tradition's evidence priorities differ markedly from CM's — there is less interest in formal assessment artifacts and more interest in the child's overall trajectory, engagement, and self-direction.

The markers below are drafted from the documented contemporary movement consensus and from Gray & Riley's published findings on what unschooling families actually look for as evidence of learning.

---

### OM-US-001 — Sustained interest pursued without external pressure

**whatItIndicates:** When a child returns to a topic, activity, or skill across days and weeks under their own initiative, with no parental prompting or reward, this is the unschooling tradition's strongest single indicator that authentic learning is happening. The duration matters less than the unprompted return. A child who plays the same game for two weeks then stops is showing the pattern just as much as a child who reads dinosaur books for six months — both are pursuing without coercion.

**markersToLookFor:**
- Child returns to a topic or activity unprompted across multiple days
- Child asks questions about it that go beyond surface curiosity
- Child seeks out additional material (books, videos, conversations, places) related to it
- Child incorporates it into play, conversation, or making
- Child's engagement is not contingent on a parent's presence or approval
- The interest may end abruptly — this is normal and not a failure

**capabilityThreadMapping:** intrinsic_motivation, sustained_attention, self_directed_learning, depth_of_engagement

**grounded_in:** SE-US-001, SE-US-007, contemporary practitioner consensus

---

### OM-US-002 — The child's overall flourishing as the primary evidence

**whatItIndicates:** The unschooling tradition treats the child's general state — emotional security, curiosity, willingness to try new things, quality of relationships, presence of joy — as the master evidence that the approach is working. This is not soft data in the unschooling frame; it is the most reliable signal available. A child who is academically "on track" but anxious, performance-focused, and afraid to be wrong is, in this tradition, showing an educational failure regardless of test scores. A child who is curious, secure, willing to try, and at home in their own life is showing educational success regardless of what they have or have not formally learned.

**markersToLookFor:**
- Child engages willingly with new situations and people
- Child is not afraid to attempt things they might fail at
- Child's emotional baseline is generally settled
- Child has trusted relationships with parents and others
- Child shows curiosity about the world rather than avoidance of it
- Child can be alone with themselves without distress
- Child's questions are open ("how does this work?") rather than performance-shaped ("am I right?")

**capabilityThreadMapping:** wellbeing, social_emotional, secure_attachment, growth_mindset

**grounded_in:** Gray & Riley 2015 findings on grown unschoolers' satisfaction; documented movement consensus; SE-US-001

---

### OM-US-003 — Self-initiated knowledge connections across domains

**whatItIndicates:** Unschooled children frequently form connections across domains in ways that schooled children, organised by subject silos, often do not. A child who connects what they noticed about insects to what they read in a fantasy novel to what they saw at the museum is demonstrating the kind of integrated thinking the unschooling tradition most prizes — not because it was instructed, but because it arose from the child's own meaning-making.

**markersToLookFor:**
- Child spontaneously references something from one domain while engaged in another
- Child notices similarities or contrasts across topics they have encountered separately
- Child applies a concept from a video game or book to a real-world situation
- Child asks integrative questions ("is that the same as the thing in...")
- Child's vocabulary is unusually broad for their age in domains they care about

**capabilityThreadMapping:** cross_domain_connection, conceptual_integration, vocabulary, transfer_of_learning

**grounded_in:** SE-US-001, contemporary practitioner consensus on integrative thinking as an unschooling outcome

---

### OM-US-004 — Capacity to direct one's own boredom

**whatItIndicates:** A child who, faced with an empty afternoon, can move through the boredom phase and arrive at their own choice of what to do is demonstrating something the unschooling tradition treats as developmentally significant. The capacity is not innate — it is built through repeated experience of being trusted to navigate boredom without rescue. Children who have always had their time structured by adults often lose this capacity and need to rebuild it during the deschooling phase.

**markersToLookFor:**
- Child can be told "I'm not going to suggest something — what do you want to do?" and eventually arrive at an answer
- The arriving sometimes takes a long time and may include complaint — both are normal
- Over weeks and months, the latency from boredom to self-direction shortens
- The child's self-chosen activity is genuinely their own, not a guess at what the parent wants
- The child does not require constant adult company to have a good day

**capabilityThreadMapping:** self_direction, autonomy, executive_function, comfort_with_uncertainty

**grounded_in:** SE-US-008 (deschooling phases); contemporary practitioner consensus on the deschooling progression

---

### OM-US-005 — Connection and trust in the parent-child relationship

**whatItIndicates:** Because unschooling depends on the child trusting the parent enough to let them in, and the parent trusting the child enough to follow rather than direct, the quality of the relationship is itself the most important capability the approach develops. The unschooling tradition treats relational trust not as an outcome of education but as the medium through which education happens. If the trust is weakening, the approach is in trouble regardless of what activities are or are not happening.

**markersToLookFor:**
- Child volunteers what they are thinking, doing, or struggling with
- Child seeks out the parent for conversation, not only for needs
- Child is honest about things they got wrong or did badly
- Child can disagree with the parent without distress
- Parent and child have inside jokes, shared references, and fluent conversation
- The parent does not feel they are losing track of who the child is

**capabilityThreadMapping:** secure_attachment, communication, trust, family_relationships

**grounded_in:** Documented contemporary practitioner consensus across Holt's legacy, Sandra Dodd's writing, Pam Laricchia's work, and Gray & Riley's findings on family relationship quality among grown unschoolers

---

### OM-US-006 — Engagement with literacy through whatever channel is available

**whatItIndicates:** In the unschooling tradition, literacy is not measured by independent reading on demand — it is measured by the child's overall embeddedness in language, story, and meaning-making, however that is occurring. A child who listens to four hours of audiobooks daily, watches captioned videos, dictates stories, and asks for long read-alouds is acquiring literacy whether or not they are technically reading the words themselves. The marker is the relationship with language, not the location of the eyes.

**markersToLookFor:**
- Child seeks out stories in any format (read-aloud, audiobook, video, conversation)
- Child has favourite authors, characters, or stories they return to
- Child can predict what comes next in a familiar story
- Child uses language from books in their own play or conversation
- Child notices print in the environment and asks about it
- Child's vocabulary growth is consistent even before independent reading emerges
- Independent reading, when it appears, does so suddenly and around something the child specifically wanted to read

**capabilityThreadMapping:** literacy, oral_language, vocabulary, narrative_comprehension

**grounded_in:** SE-US-003 (range of reading ages); PP-US-002

## Layer 4 — Facilitation Vocabulary

**pedagogyKey:** unschooling
**layer:** facilitationVocabulary
**status:** v1 partial — commissioned author should expand each section significantly

### Verbs the tradition uses for what the parent does

- **Strew.** Place something interesting in the child's path with no instruction, no expectation, and no follow-up. The strewing is complete the moment the item is placed.
- **Follow the interest.** When a child shows engagement with something, support it without redirecting it. If the child is interested in trains, get more train books, find a train museum, watch train videos together. When the interest fades, let it fade.
- **Trust the child.** Operationally, this means not intervening when intervention is not asked for, not testing comprehension, not requiring demonstration of learning, and not assuming the child needs a structure they have not asked for.
- **Be a partner.** Engage as a co-explorer rather than as a teacher. Answer questions truthfully. Say "I don't know — let's find out" when you don't know. Share what you find interesting without requiring the child to find it interesting.
- **Connect.** When the child wants something the parent cannot provide directly — a topic the parent doesn't know, a skill the parent doesn't have, a community the parent isn't part of — the parent's job is to find the connection, not to become the expert.
- **Deschool yourself.** The most operational verb in the tradition. The parent's own internalised assumptions about what learning looks like are the primary obstacle to unschooling working, and dismantling those assumptions is ongoing work.

### Characteristic restraints

- Do not test what the child has learned. The testing changes what learning is.
- Do not offer rewards for engaging with anything. The reward changes the engagement.
- Do not impose limits as a generic policy. Make decisions case by case in dialogue with the child.
- Do not manufacture interest where none exists. If the child is bored by a thing, the thing is boring to them right now.
- Do not catch up on what schooled children "should know" by a certain age. The "should" is the school's, not the child's.
- Do not perform unschooling for relatives, social media, or yourself. Authenticity is the load-bearing element.

### Example micro-scripts

**When the child asks a question:**
"Hmm, I don't know. Want to look it up together?"

**When the child shows you something:**
"Tell me about it." (Not "good job.")

**When the child is bored:**
"Yeah, sometimes there's nothing happening. I'm here if you want to do something together, otherwise I'll be in the kitchen."

**When the child wants to do something you find tedious:**
"Sure." (And then actually do it without sighing.)

**When the child wants to stop something:**
"OK." (Not "are you sure? You've put so much work into it.")

**When a relative asks what the child is learning:**
"All sorts of things. He's really into [authentic current interest] right now."

**v1_note:** The commissioned author should expand each section to at least twice this length, with age-specific scripts, scripts for shared activities with multiple children, and scripts for moments of parental disagreement with the child's choice.

## Layer 5 — Contraindications and Tensions

The unschooling tradition has internal tensions that the corpus must represent honestly. A v1 sketch of five; Wave 5 target ~8.

---

### CI-US-001 — Radical vs. relaxed unschooling

**warned_against (from radical position):** Calling oneself an unschooler while imposing time limits, requiring specific activities, or controlling food, sleep, or screens.

**tradition_reasoning:** The radical position, articulated forcefully by writers including Sandra Dodd and Idzie Desmarais, holds that unschooling is a single coherent philosophy: trust the child across all of life, not just academics. To restrict in some areas while not restricting in others, in this view, undermines the foundation of trust that makes the whole approach work. The child experiences the inconsistency as conditional trust.

**counter_position:** Relaxed or moderate unschoolers — including many practitioners who would still use the term unschooling — accept some areas of parental decision-making (sleep, food, sometimes screens) while extending child autonomy in education and most daily choices. They argue that radical unschooling exceeds what is responsible in a contemporary household with safety, health, and family-rhythm considerations.

**honest_status:** This is an unresolved internal disagreement. Neither position has empirical support over the other. Hearth families calling themselves unschoolers should know the disagreement exists and locate themselves within it consciously rather than accidentally.

**grounded_in:** Documented in writings by Sandra Dodd, Idzie Desmarais, Pam Laricchia, and others.

---

### CI-US-002 — The screen time debate

**warned_against (from no-limits position):** Imposing screen time limits.

**tradition_reasoning:** The no-limits position holds that screens contain genuine learning, that limits create hoarding, that restriction signals distrust, and that children given unrestricted access typically self-regulate over time. This is the dominant published position among the most prominent contemporary unschooling writers.

**counter_position:** A minority position within the movement, and a much larger position among adjacent self-directed learning families, holds that contemporary digital design contains attention-hijacking elements that did not exist in earlier generations of media, that some children show genuine compulsive patterns, and that complete non-intervention can cause harm.

**honest_status:** Empirical evidence is genuinely lacking on both sides. The debate is the most heated within the movement. Hearth's framing should present both positions without resolving them.

**grounded_in:** Contemporary movement debate; documented in Sandra Dodd, Joyce Fetteroll, Pam Laricchia, and Sue Patterson's writings (favouring no limits) and in moderate-unschooling and self-directed-education adjacent voices (favouring some limits).

---

### CI-US-003 — Children with learning differences

**warned_against (from purist position):** Introducing specialist intervention for reading difficulties, math difficulties, or other learning differences before the child has had the chance to develop on their own timeline.

**tradition_reasoning:** The purist position holds that all children develop at different rates, that "learning disability" is often a label imposed by school's narrow timeline, and that many children diagnosed in school environments would have been fine if simply allowed to develop without comparison.

**counter_position:** Specialists, and many experienced unschooling parents whose children have actual diagnoses, hold that some children genuinely do not learn certain skills without targeted, structured intervention — that a child with dyslexia, dysgraphia, or dyscalculia may need explicit teaching that the unschooling approach does not provide on its own. The honest position is that pure non-intervention can leave a child without skills they needed and would have wanted.

**honest_status:** This is the contraindication most likely to cause real harm if applied dogmatically. Hearth should present it explicitly. A child who appears to be struggling in a way that goes beyond developmental variation deserves assessment and potentially targeted support, and that does not have to mean abandoning the unschooling approach in other areas.

**grounded_in:** Movement debate documented across multiple practitioners; specialist literature on dyslexia and specific learning differences.

---

### CI-US-004 — Privilege and access

**warned_against:** Treating unschooling as universally available without acknowledging the conditions it depends on.

**tradition_reasoning:** Honest unschooling writers, including Akilah Richards and Jody Allard, have noted that successful unschooling typically depends on at least one parent being available much of the time, on a literacy-rich home environment, on access to community and resources, on financial stability, and often on cultural capital that schools normally transmit. Families without these conditions can still unschool, but the approach does not transcend material circumstances.

**honest_status:** This is increasingly recognised within the movement and should not be hidden in Hearth's framing. The Australian context is more accessible than the US in some respects (smaller class sizes are not the alternative; the homeschool community is well-organised; registration is accessible) but the privilege critique still applies.

**grounded_in:** Akilah Richards's writing, Jody Allard's published essays on intellectual privilege, Morrison (2018) academic work.

---

### CI-US-005 — Performing unschooling vs. living it

**warned_against:** Treating unschooling as an identity to perform on social media, in conversation, or to oneself, rather than a daily practice in the actual home.

**tradition_reasoning:** Multiple practitioners have noted that the most common failure mode of unschooling is not the child but the parent — specifically, the parent who has adopted the language and identity of unschooling but is still operating from the anxiety and control patterns that unschooling is meant to dissolve. The child experiences the gap between the language and the reality.

**honest_status:** This is a contraindication directed at the parent's inner state rather than at any specific practice. It shows up as families who say all the right things but whose children are anxious, compliant in school-shaped ways, or visibly performing for the parent.

**grounded_in:** Contemporary practitioner consensus on parent self-work as the primary skill of unschooling.

---

The commissioned author should add: pursuing unschooling through adolescence into university transition; the question of whether unschooling is compatible with single-parent or both-parents-working households; and the specific failure pattern of "unschooling out of laziness" — i.e., calling it unschooling when it is actually disengagement.*

## Layer 6 — Worked Examples

### WE-US-001

**scenario:** Eli (9) has been "doing nothing" for three weeks. The family deschooled out of conventional school four months ago. He spent the first month happy and exploring, the second month bored and complaining, and the third month playing the same video game for hours every day. The mother is starting to feel like she made a mistake leaving school. She's logging this entry with worry.

**capability_threads:** self_direction, intrinsic_motivation, sustained_attention
**age_range:** 8_to_10
**activity_type:** deschooling_progression

**interpretation_in_unschooling_voice:**

This is the deschooling progression, exactly as the practitioners describe it. The first month of celebration, the second month of boredom, and now the third month — and what looks like "the same video game for hours every day" is, in this tradition, almost certainly Eli doing real work. The mother's instinct to read it as failure is the school-conditioned anxiety doing its job.

A few specific things would help here. First, the mother should sit beside Eli for an hour and ask him about the game — not testing him, just genuinely curious. Most parents who do this come away surprised at how much is actually happening: planning, problem-solving, reading menus and instructions, often communication with other players, often substantial mathematics in resource management. The game is not "nothing." Naming what is in it is the first step out of the false framing.

Second, the mother should look at the rest of life and ask whether it is genuinely interesting and available right now. Not "is it educational" — is it interesting? A trip to a place worth visiting, a meal with people worth eating with, a half-built thing in the kitchen the child can join. Not as covert intervention to lure Eli off the screen, but because if the rest of life is thin, the screen is the only place anything is happening, and that is the parent's territory to fix.

Third, and most importantly: the mother should hold her nerve. Four months in is early. The Gray and Riley research, the practitioner consensus, the testimonies of grown unschoolers — all of it points to this period being normal and not predictive. Children in deschooling do not progress on a tidy curve. The mother who panics here and re-imposes structure typically resets the whole clock. The mother who holds steady through the third and fourth months almost always sees a fifth month that looks materially different.

The most useful specific thing the mother could do this week: stop logging "Eli did nothing today" in her own head, and start logging "Eli played [specific game] for [duration], appeared [emotional state], we [whatever shared time happened]." The shift in framing is half the work.

**grounded_in:** SE-US-008 (deschooling phases), SE-US-002 (parent challenges), PP-US-001 (gaming), OM-US-004 (boredom navigation).

### WE-US-002

**scenario:** Mia (12) is being unschooled in NSW. The annual Authorised Person home visit is in two weeks. The mother has been keeping informal notes but feels her documentation is "thin" and is panicking. She's logging an entry asking for help putting together a learning record that will satisfy the AP without misrepresenting how Mia actually spends her days.

**capability_threads:** parent_self_management, regulatory_compliance, documentation
**age_range:** 11_to_13
**activity_type:** registration_preparation

**interpretation_in_unschooling_voice:**

The mother's panic is the right thing to address first, because panicked documentation reads as panicked documentation and that is what makes AP visits go badly. The good news is that NSW has been registering unschooling families successfully for decades — practitioners with much "thinner" documentation than the mother thinks she has have walked through this many times.

Operationally, the next two weeks should look like this:

**Week one: gather what already exists.** Phone photos, sketchbook contents, library checkout history, screen time spent on games and websites the mother can identify, books read aloud, conversations the mother remembers, places visited. This is almost always more than the mother thinks. Spread it across the kitchen table physically. Most mothers in this situation discover they have an entire term of evidence, just not in the form they were imagining.

**Week one, second pass: translate into curriculum-area language.** Take each piece of evidence and ask which Australian Curriculum learning area or NESA syllabus outcome it speaks to. A Minecraft city is design and digital technologies plus mathematics plus literacy if Mia is reading instructions plus arguably civics if she is moderating a server. A library run with three novels is English. A morning at the museum bug exhibit is science. A long conversation about why grandma is sad is health and personal development. The translation is not invention — it is the same events, named in the framework's vocabulary.

**Week two: write the learning record as a brief narrative organised by learning area, not by date.** The AP wants to see that learning is happening across the breadth of the curriculum. A two-page document with three to five examples per learning area, each example specific and dated, is dramatically more compelling than a daily log. Quote Mia where possible. Include photos.

**Week two, the day before the visit: brief Mia gently and once.** "An adult is coming tomorrow to chat with us about how we learn. You don't need to do anything special — just be yourself. If she asks you what you've been doing, tell her honestly." That is the entire prep. No coaching, no rehearsal, no anxiety projected onto Mia. The AP wants to meet a real twelve-year-old, not a performance.

**During the visit:** the mother should be calm, the documentation should be on the table, and the conversation should be allowed to happen. APs are people, not auditors. They are looking for evidence that the child is learning and being treated well, both of which are true.

The practitioner who has done this thirty-seven times across her own children and in coaching others is Sue Elvis. Her published material on NSW visits is the most useful single resource on this specific topic. Hearth families in NSW should be pointed to it directly.

**grounded_in:** PP-US-003 (Australian regulatory documentation), Sue Elvis published work, Freya Dawson NSW analysis, BarefootChild QLD examples for cross-jurisdiction reference.
