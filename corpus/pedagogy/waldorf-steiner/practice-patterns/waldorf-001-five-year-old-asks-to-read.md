## Trigger

A child of around five asks the parent to teach them to read — often after a cousin or a neighbour's child has started Prep and come home with sight-word lists, or after a grandparent has asked whether the child "knows their letters yet." The parent is drawn to Waldorf's protected early years but does not want to refuse a child who is asking, and is unsure whether the request is a readiness signal that overrides the tradition's timing.

## Response

The Waldorf tradition reads this request first as imitation and belonging, not as the intellect announcing itself ready for work. The five-year-old wants to do what the big people do and what the school cousin is doing; the request is the child reaching for the adult world, which is exactly what a child of this age is built to do. The tradition does not read it as evidence that the forces of thinking have been freed for abstract work — in Steiner's picture those forces are still busy building the body until the change of teeth, and the second teeth, not the request, are the sign.

What the tradition values, then, is honouring the reach without feeding it the abstract sign. A Waldorf parent would typically answer warmly and without refusal — "You will learn to read; your letters are on their way to you" — and then meet the appetite with the things that build toward reading in this tradition: stories told aloud, night after night, the same ones; being read to; rhymes, songs and clapping games that carry rhythm and the sounds of language into the body; drawing, painting and modelling that train the hand and the eye for form; and plenty of seeing the adults in the house read and write for real reasons, so that literacy stays something the big people visibly do. A child who starts "writing" letters of their own invention, or who begins to pick words off the Weet-Bix box, is not stopped and not drilled; the tradition would let it ripen, treating the child's own picking-up as the environment working rather than as a cue to start a programme.

When letters do come, around the change of teeth, the tradition brings them through pictures and story — the letter drawn out of the wave or the mountain or the fish before it is read — and through writing before reading, so that the sign is something the child's hand has made. The reading that follows is, in the tradition's reading, no slower for the wait and a good deal healthier: the forces that were left to finish the body are available for it, and the child arrives at letters with pictures behind them rather than with a drilled alphabet and no inner life for it to live in. The tradition would also note that the parent's own calm matters more than the answer: a child who asks to read and is met with anxiety about Prep comparisons absorbs the anxiety, which is the one thing the tradition would most want kept out of the room.

## Anti-pattern

Treating the request as a readiness signal and starting a phonics programme or a reading app. Making reading a forbidden thing, which turns it into a desired object and teaches the child that the parent is withholding. Explaining to the five-year-old why they can't learn yet — an abstract explanation about development is itself the thing the tradition is protecting the child from. Correcting or testing the child's own invented writing. Letting a grandparent's comparison set the pace.
