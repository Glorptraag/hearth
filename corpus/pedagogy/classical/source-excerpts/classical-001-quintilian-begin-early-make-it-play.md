## Text

Quintilian opens his account of education by telling a father to expect the best of his newborn son, because the common complaint that only a few people are able to learn is simply false: most children take in what they are taught readily and quickly, and the genuinely slow or unteachable child is about as rare as a child born malformed. Quickness of mind, he argues, is as natural to a human being as flight is to a bird.

On when to begin, he rejects the view that nothing should be attempted before the age of seven. Even the earliest years, he says, should not be allowed to go to waste — and all the more because the first elements of learning rest on memory alone, and memory in a small child is not merely present but at its sharpest. He is careful, though, about what beginning early does not mean. The child of three or four is not to be driven, nor made to produce a full measure of work. The overriding concern is that the child must not come to hate learning before they are old enough to love it, carrying that early bitterness forward into later years. So the lessons of the very young are to be made a kind of play: the child is to be asked questions and praised for answering; is never to be left comfortable with not knowing; may sometimes watch another child being taught, so as to feel a spark of rivalry; should be allowed to win now and then and to believe the win was earned; and should be drawn on by the small rewards that children of that age actually care about.

## Context

Original Hearth paraphrase of Watson's translation of Institutio Oratoria I.1.1–3 (nearly every child can learn; the unteachable are rare) and I.1.15–20 (begin before seven; memory most tenacious in childhood; make instruction a pleasure; question, praise, rivalry, rewards). The registered archive.org scan, Project Gutenberg, Wikisource and the Wayback Machine were all blocked by the egress policy of the authoring session, so Watson's exact wording could not be checked against the page. Phrases I am confident appear in Watson, because exact-phrase search placed them on pages hosting his translation: "dull and unteachable persons are no more produced in the course of nature than are persons marked by monstrosity and deformities" (I.1.2) and "Let us not then lose even the earliest period of life", with memory "at that time of life even most tenacious" (I.1.19). I recall I.1.20 as "Let his instruction be an amusement to him; let him be questioned and praised…" but could not confirm that string, so the entire entry is a paraphrase rather than a verbatim claim.
