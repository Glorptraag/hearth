## Text

For the years before the change of teeth Steiner offers two guiding words: imitation and example. The small child does not learn from what we tell it; it learns from what it sees done. Everything that happens within reach of the senses — our movements, our tone, our gestures of care or carelessness, the moral quality of what we do as much as the physical shape of it — is taken in and copied, and in the copying the child's own organs are formed into the shapes they will keep. He gives a homely illustration: a child who takes money from a drawer is not showing a bad character but imitating what it has watched a parent do every day. It follows that moral instruction is nearly useless at this age and the adult's conduct is nearly everything. The rule Steiner draws is that nothing should be done in the child's presence that the child ought not to imitate.

Because the child absorbs the mood of the house as readily as its deeds, Steiner asks for joy, warmth and honest, unforced affection around the young child. He treats these as formative forces — a glad face and a loving atmosphere help shape the growing body rightly, while a sour or anxious one does its own quiet harm. The same reasoning governs playthings. A doll made from a folded napkin with two ink dots for eyes asks the child's imagination to supply the rest, and in supplying it the child's inner picture-making is exercised and the brain is built; a finished, pretty doll leaves the child nothing to do, and the forces that would have done it lie idle. For Steiner the imaginative work of the young child is not a diversion from development but one of its engines.
