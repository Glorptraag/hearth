## Trigger

Parent is logging concern about a child (any age, but most commonly 7–14) spending what feels like excessive time on video games. The parent is anxious that this is "not learning" and feels social pressure from family or friends to impose limits.

## Response

The unschooling response to gaming runs through three distinct ideas the parent needs to hear in order. The order matters because parents who hear only the third one without the first two often feel the answer has bypassed their actual concern.

First: in unschooling thought, video games are legitimate learning environments. They contain reading (often substantial, often technical), problem-solving, spatial reasoning, planning, resource management, social negotiation, persistence through failure, and frequently a community of practice. The parent who has never engaged with the specific game their child is playing is in a poor position to assess what it contains. The first move is not to limit but to learn — sit beside the child for an hour, ask what they're doing, watch how they think.

Second: when parents have previously restricted games, removal of those restrictions almost always produces a period of compensatory gaming. This looks like addiction, but in the unschooling frame it is more usefully understood as hoarding — the response of any human to a previously-rationed resource suddenly becoming abundant. The hoarding period typically resolves itself over weeks or months as the child learns the resource will not be taken away. Restricting gaming during this period prolongs the hoarding indefinitely; trusting through it lets it complete.

Third: the parent's discomfort with gaming is often less about the gaming and more about a fear that the child is opting out of "real life." The unschooling response is to ensure the rest of life is genuinely available and genuinely interesting — not as a covert intervention to lure the child off the screen, but because a thin offline life makes the screen the only place where anything is happening. Strewing other interesting things, going to interesting places, eating real meals together, making the home a place worth being in — all of this is the parent's actual job here. None of it is enforcement.

A note specific to families with relatives or in-laws who are critical: the parent's task is also to absorb that pressure rather than transmit it to the child. The child should not pay the cost of grandma's discomfort.

## Anti-pattern

Do not impose time limits as a unilateral parental decision. Do not require "earning" screen time through chores or schoolwork. Do not characterise gaming to the child as a problem to be managed. Do not, when the child is mid-game, deliver lectures about the importance of other activities. All of these reinforce the dynamic the unschooling approach is trying to dissolve.
