## What it indicates

When a child, on their own initiative, goes and gets what they need to go further — pulls a particular book off the shelf, searches for the video that explains the exact thing, rings a grandparent who used to do that job, asks the parent to find someone who knows — the unschooling tradition reads this as evidence that the learning has become the child's own project. The content of the interest matters less than the move. The child has located a gap in what they know and acted to close it. In a tradition with no teacher assigning the next step, this is what the next step looks like when it happens.

The tradition distinguishes this sharply from two things it can resemble. It is not the child asking to be entertained (that is boredom, and this corpus reads it separately), and it is not dependence. "Can you find me a book about volcanoes that shows the inside" is a competent act: the parent is being used as a resource, which is exactly the role the tradition wants the parent in. Holt's observation was that children direct their own learning when they are trusted to; Illich's picture of education was a web of things, skills and people that a learner reaches for, rather than a curriculum delivered on a schedule. A child reaching deliberately is enacting both. Strewing is the parent's half of this exchange — placing interesting things in the path with no expectation attached. Resource-seeking is the child's half. When both halves are visible, the exchange is working.

Grown unschoolers surveyed by Gray and Riley (2015, Reports I and II) described knowing how to find what they needed — a book, a mentor, a course, a person to ask — as the capacity that carried them into further study and work, and several reported being comfortable approaching lecturers and other adults because they had grown up treating adults as people to ask rather than authorities to perform for. The marker in a nine-year-old and the capacity in a twenty-five-year-old are the same thing at two ages.

For an Australian parent keeping notes for registration, this is one of the easiest markers to document honestly. "She asked for a library book on X and read it that afternoon" or "he messaged his uncle to ask how welding works" are real events, dated, in the child's own direction, and they map onto the curriculum's language about independent inquiry without any strain in the translation.

## Look for

- The ask is specific: a particular book, the video that explains the exact thing, the person who actually knows — not "find me something to do"
- The child locates the resource themselves where they can (the library shelf, a search, a sibling, a neighbour) and asks for help only where they genuinely cannot reach it (transport, money, an adult's contact)
- The child returns to the resource once it is in hand — it was wanted, not just requested
- The child asks the parent to connect them with a person (a grandparent, a tradesperson, a club, an online community) and is willing to do the talking themselves
- The child tolerates a resource not having the answer and tries another route rather than abandoning the question
- Over months, the range of places the child knows to look widens — books, then videos, then people, then institutions (the library's inter-library loan desk, the museum, a TAFE open day)
- Asking for help arrives without apology or embarrassment — in this tradition that ease is itself the marker, not a lack of independence
