## What it indicates

When a child under about seven spontaneously takes up adult work in play — sweeping, cooking, hanging washing, hammering, "going to work", loading a barrow — the Waldorf tradition reads it as the clearest available sign that the home is being absorbed and that the child's will is engaged and healthy. Before the change of teeth the child learns by imitation, and imitation shows up not as a lesson repeated back but as a deed re-enacted, usually hours or days later and in the child's own form. The result of the "work" does not matter and the child does not mind; the deed is the point, and the whole body is in it. The marker also reads backwards onto the household: it tells the parent that their own work is visible, unhurried enough to be watched, and worth copying — which, in this tradition, is the curriculum of the early years doing its job. Where imitation is absent, and a young child only consumes entertainment or asks to be entertained, the tradition would not look at the child first but at what the adults are doing within the child's sight.

## Look for

- The play follows real work the child has seen, often the same day: the morning's gardening reappears as the afternoon's farming
- Whole-body involvement — carrying, sweeping, stirring, digging, pushing — rather than talking about the task
- Absorption without an audience; the child does not look up for approval and may not want the adult to join
- Gestures, phrases and tone copied from the adult, sometimes uncomfortably exactly — the sigh, the hat, the "right, one more and then a cuppa"
- Repetition across days, with the play deepening and gaining detail rather than changing subject
- No product, and no distress about the lack of one; the loaded barrow is tipped and refilled for the pleasure of the loading
- Imitation of the adult's mood as well as their task — a sign the atmosphere of the house is being absorbed along with its deeds
