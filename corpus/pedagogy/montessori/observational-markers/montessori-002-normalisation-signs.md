## What it indicates

"Normalisation" is the name Montessori gave in her later books to the state a child settles into when the environment has been meeting him for a while; in her 1912 and 1914 writing she calls the same thing spontaneous discipline and says it is "the most striking result of our method". It is a convergence of several things at once: concentration on self-chosen work, a love of order and of work itself, self-discipline that nobody is enforcing, and a particular kind of sociability — delight rather than jealousy at another child's success, an obliging obedience that does what is asked and then goes straight back to the interrupted work. The children in a settled Children's House, she wrote, "have shown a love of work which no one suspected to be in them, and a calm and an orderliness in their movements"; forty of them could work for hours with hardly a word, each intent on his own task, and "it never happens that quarrels arise over the possession of an object".

Two things keep this marker honest. First, it is a state, not a trait: it comes and goes, and it is read as evidence about the match between child and environment rather than as the child being "good". Second, it is not the same as a quiet, compliant child. Montessori is sharp about this — a child made "artificially silent as a mute and as immovable as a paralytic" is "annihilated, not disciplined", and "a calm man and a self-disciplined man are not one and the same". The quiet of normalisation arises from activity that has found its order; quiet produced by adult pressure, or compliance that evaporates when the adult leaves the room, is a different phenomenon and the tradition does not mistake one for the other. Parents new to the method often misread the real thing as "too subdued", or assume that once it has appeared it should be permanent.

## Look for

- Chooses work without being directed, settles quickly, and changes it "according to his inner needs" rather than at an adult's suggestion
- Long stretches of absorbed work; a morning where hours pass with few words
- Materials handled carefully and returned to their places; spills wiped and rugs straightened without reminders
- No quarrels over materials; a sibling's achievement greeted with interest or imitation rather than envy
- Obedience that arrives easily to a quiet word or gesture — and a return to the interrupted work once the request is met
- Bright, unafraid, cordial with visitors; eager to show work without depending on praise for it
- Over weeks, outbursts of rage giving way to what Montessori calls "outbursts of joy"
- Not counted: stillness produced by pressure, or good behaviour that disappears when the adult steps out
