## Verbs

- **Hold the rhythm** — Keep the shape of the day, the week and the year steady enough that the child can lean on it: the same morning work, the same walk, the same story-time, bread on Tuesday, the same verse before the meal. Rhythm is not a timetable. It is a breathing — in and out, gathered and free, quiet and loud — repeated until the child's body knows what comes next without being told. Most of what a Waldorf parent "teaches" before seven is carried by rhythm rather than by words.
- **Model** — Do the real work of the household in the child's sight, with care and without commentary, and let the child's imitation do the rest. Before the change of teeth the adult's deeds and the adult's inner attitude are the curriculum. The parent sweeping with attention teaches more than any lesson about tidiness.
- **Do alongside** — Give the child a real version of the work rather than a toy version or an explanation of it: a small broom, a blunt knife and a soft vegetable, a basket of pegs. Work side by side. The child is not helping; the child is becoming someone who works.
- **Tell (not explain)** — Carry teaching in a story, a verse, a song or a picture rather than in an explanation. A told story — eyes on the child, no book in hand — lets the child build the pictures inside, and the pictures are the point. Explanation hands the child a finished concept and leaves nothing for the inner life to do.
- **Bring the picture** — When the child asks why, answer with an image that is true to the thing: the tree letting its leaves go for its long winter sleep; the bread rising because the little yeast is breathing. Let the picture do the work the abstract concept will do later, around nine or ten, when the child can take it.
- **Let it ripen** — Trust that what has gone in through the senses and the limbs will come out in its own time as play, as drawing, as a story retold on a walk. Do not ask for it back the same day. Do not test for it at all. What ripens slowly is held longest.
- **Breathe out** — When the day tightens — the child wild, the parent sharp — read it as a rhythm that has lost its out-breath, not as a discipline problem. Go outside, dig, run, carry heavy things, climb. Then gather again around food or a candle or a story. The pattern is in-breath, out-breath, in-breath, and most unsettled afternoons are a missed out-breath.
- **Warm the home** — Keep the child warm in the plain physical sense (wool, soup, socks) and in the felt sense: an unhurried voice, a face that is glad to see them, beauty in small things — a cloth on the table, a seasonal corner with a gum nut and a feather on it. Steiner treats warmth and joy as forces that shape the growing body, not as niceties.

## Restraints

- Do not explain what a picture can carry. If the story has done its work, the lesson has landed; the explanation afterwards takes it back out of the child's hands.
- Do not ask the young child to reflect on their feelings or their learning. "How did that make you feel?" and "What did you learn today?" pull a child under seven out of their dream and into a self-consciousness that is not yet theirs.
- Do not rush letters and numbers. Reading, writing and sums come after the change of teeth, through drawing, story and movement first. A child who learns to read at seven and a half has lost nothing; a child drilled at four has spent forces that were busy elsewhere.
- Do not introduce screens or recorded media in the early years. The child before seven takes in the world through imitation of living people; a screen offers nothing to imitate and a great deal to absorb.
- Do not quiz after a story. No comprehension questions, no "what was the wolf's name?" The story is left whole, and tomorrow night it is told again.
- Do not correct the imitation. When the child sweeps badly, cooks a mess, or hammers the wrong thing, the deed is what matters, not the result. Keep working beside them and let the quality follow in its own years.
- Do not hand the young child the day's decisions. "What do you want to do now?" is a weight, not a freedom, before seven. The rhythm decides, and the child is carried by it.
- Do not announce transitions with instructions when a song or a verse will carry them. The same tidy-up song every day moves a four-year-old more gently than any amount of "right, time to pack up."
- Do not hurry what is quietly ripening. A child retelling yesterday's story in their own words, or playing out the morning's work all afternoon, is finishing something — leave it to finish.

## Micro-scripts

- **When the five-year-old asks why the leaves are falling** — "The tree is getting ready for its long winter sleep. It lets its leaves go the way you let your boots go at the door." (A picture, not a lesson on chlorophyll. The explanation can come at ten.)
- **When the morning has gone sideways and everyone is scratchy** — (No speech about it. The parent puts on boots and starts the tidy-up song, or says) "Boots on, we're going out to the back paddock." (Breathe out first; gather again afterwards with food.)
- **When the five-year-old asks to be taught to read** — "You will learn to read — your letters are on their way to you. Tonight we'll have the story of the three billy goats again, and tomorrow you can draw me the bridge." (Warm, unhurried, no refusal and no programme.)
- **When the child is sweeping beside you, badly** — (Nothing. Keep sweeping. At the end, hand over the dustpan and let them tip it into the bin.)
- **At the end of the evening story** — (Silence. The candle is blown out or the lamp dimmed. No questions, no moral. The same story again tomorrow night.)
- **When the child asks a hard question about death or where the baby came from** — "That is a big one. Let me tell you a story about it on our walk tomorrow." (And then do — a picture the parent actually believes, not a clever comparison.)
- **Before the meal, every day** — (The same short verse of thanks, said together, hands still. The words matter less than the sameness.)
