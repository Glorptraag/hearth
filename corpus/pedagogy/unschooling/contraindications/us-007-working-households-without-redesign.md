## Reasoning

Unschooling is sometimes described — by critics, and occasionally by its own newcomers — as "not doing school", which makes it sound like the one approach a busy household can run with no one home. The movement's own practitioners have said for decades that this is exactly backwards. Sandra Dodd's formulation, repeated across the community in various words, is that unschooling is not un-parenting: it replaces the school's structure with the parent's presence, and presence is the expensive part. Joyce Fetteroll and Pam Laricchia both describe the parent's work as being with the children — noticing, conversing, answering, driving, finding the person who knows — and both are clear that this takes more adult attention than a workbook, not less.

What the day actually runs on. In an unschooling household the infrastructure is invisible precisely because it is not scheduled: an adult who is around when the question arrives and still around twenty minutes later when it turns into something; strewing, which only works if someone is there to notice what landed; conversation at the kitchen bench as the main medium of learning; transport to the library, the creek, the museum, the friend's house, the TAFE open day; the money and the time to say yes to the thing the child reached for. Remove the adult and what remains is not unschooling. It is children at home with a screen, and the tradition is honest that this is a different thing with different outcomes.

What the evidence says, reported in Hearth's words. In Gray and Riley's 2013 survey of 232 unschooling families (CC BY-NC-ND — cited here, not reproduced), the respondents were predominantly households with a parent, almost always the mother, at home, and among the challenges they named were the time and energy the approach demands of that parent, the loss of a second income, and the difficulty of finding time for themselves — alongside the better-known challenge of their own deschooling. The shape of the sample is itself informative: unschooling as practised and reported was largely an at-home-parent phenomenon, which says less about whether other households can do it than about how much redesign they need. Gray and Riley's 2015 Report I adds the other half: the few grown unschoolers who looked back on their upbringing unhappily described homes marked by parental depression and disengagement — not homes with a plan the parents were too busy to follow, but homes where no adult was really there.

What redesign looks like when it is done honestly. Families who make unschooling work with two incomes or one parent do not do it by hoping. They shift hours so that one adult is present for most of the waking day, or share the week across two adults, grandparents and a trusted friend or two. They move the family's "day" to evenings and weekends when that is when the adult is available. They use the car trip, the shift-work mornings, the shop or the workplace itself as places where conversation and real work happen. They lean harder on community — co-ops, homeschool groups, a mentor who takes the child one day a week — than a stay-at-home household needs to. And they are frank with themselves about which hours the child is genuinely accompanied and which hours they are simply unsupervised. Australian registration places responsibility for the programme on the parent personally; a plan with no adult in it for most of the day is one a reviewer can reasonably ask about, and should.

Counter-position within the movement: some practitioners point out, fairly, that older children and teenagers need far less live adult presence than this implies; that a fourteen-year-old with a library card, a bus pass and a parent who is fully present from five o'clock can unschool well; and that the stay-at-home-mother picture of unschooling is itself something the movement's privilege critique has begun to question. That is right, and it is why this warning is about redesign rather than prohibition.

Honest status: unschooling does not transcend the household's material circumstances — this corpus already says so — and the household with no adult home is where the gap between the philosophy and the practice is widest. A single parent or two working parents can unschool. They cannot do it by subtraction. If the day has not been rebuilt around when an adult is actually there, the children are not being trusted; they are being left, and the tradition itself would be the first to name the difference.

Tension with other traditions: every home-based tradition assumes an adult, but they spend that adult's time differently, and this matters to a time-poor household choosing between them. Montessori's prepared environment is designed so that the environment carries part of the load — a child can work productively in a well-prepared room with the adult observing rather than engaging. Charlotte Mason's short morning lessons and free afternoons concentrate the adult's active work into a couple of hours. Unschooling is the most conversation-dependent of the five and so the most sensitive to an adult's absence. An Eclectic family with limited adult hours is choosing between coherent designs that spend adult time in different ways, not between more and less effort.
