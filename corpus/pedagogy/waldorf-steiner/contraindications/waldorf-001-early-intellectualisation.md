## Reasoning

Steiner's warning rests on his account of the first seven years. The forces that will later serve thinking are, before the change of teeth, busy building and shaping the body; they are not yet free, and the sign that they have become free is the second teeth, not the child's cleverness. Drawing those forces off into the head early — through letters and numbers drilled at four, explanations where a picture would do, questions that ask the young child to reflect on themselves, "educational" toys that leave nothing to the imagination — weakens the body-building work and, in his account, produces a thin, nervous, prematurely clever child whose forces are not there when they are wanted later. Imagination is not the alternative to development in these years; it is one of its engines. The napkin doll with ink-dot eyes builds the brain precisely because the child must supply the rest, and the fairy tale told again and again does its work because the child builds the pictures inside. The tradition's shorthand for all this is "will before intellect": the young child learns through the limbs and through doing, and the thinking that is pressed before its time is bought at the cost of something else.

Implication for Hearth: a Waldorf family logging a five-year-old's elaborate pretend — the farm, the shop, the long game of being the mother — should receive insights that value the imaginative picture and the will-activity in the tradition's own terms, not framing that counts the play as "pre-literacy" or "pre-numeracy" gains, suggests a letter or counting extension, or praises the child for being advanced. The overlay language can quietly pull a family out of its chosen tradition, and the pull toward early academics is the strongest one in the Australian environment, where Prep comparisons and grandparents' questions about letters arrive early.

Tension with other traditions: Montessori takes the opposite position on the same years, and does so for coherent reasons of her own. Montessori treats adult-introduced fantasy before six with suspicion because she sees the young child as working hard to tell reality from unreality, and reads fairy tales and elaborate pretend as interference with that work; she prefers real objects, real tasks and true narratives about real things. On literacy she reads the child's interest in letters at around four or five as a sensitive period to be met — sandpaper letters, the movable alphabet — rather than a force to be protected from use. Steiner starts from the body-building work of the first seven years and the picture-consciousness of the young child; Montessori starts from the absorbent mind and the observed sensitive periods. Both are internally consistent, and they recommend different things for the same four-year-old. An Eclectic family drawn to both should understand that they are choosing between two theories of what the young child's mind is doing, not splitting the difference.
