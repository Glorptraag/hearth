## Text

Having described how a tutor reads a pupil, Quintilian turns to what the tutor must not do. Every learner, he says, needs rest. Nothing bears unbroken strain — even things without life or feeling lose their strength unless they are allowed to slacken — and in any case study depends on the learner's own willingness, which no one can compel. A child who comes back from a break comes back fresher and keener. He does not want that rest to be unlimited: refuse it altogether and the child learns to hate the work; grant too much and the child learns idleness. But play he positively welcomes in the young, as a sign of a lively spirit; he would not expect a child who is listless even at play to be eager at a book. He reads a child's games as a window on character, and recommends the little question-and-answer contests that sharpen the wits.

Then the famous refusal. Beating children, however customary, he will not approve. His reasons are three. It is degrading, a treatment fit for slaves, and plainly an insult if one imagines it done to an older person. A child so dull that correction by words does not reach them will simply harden to blows, as the worst slaves do. And a tutor who actually attends to the child's work day by day will never need it — beating, he observes, is usually the remedy for a tutor's own neglect, the child punished afterwards for not having done what no one made sure they did. He adds two further objections. A child who has only ever been driven by blows will one day be a young person who cannot be beaten and who has harder things still to learn — and what will drive them then? And the licence given to those who punish has too often been the cover for shameful treatment of children; he will not dwell on it, he says, because what is already understood is too much.

## Context

Original Hearth paraphrase of I.3.8–12 (relaxation; the will cannot be forced; play as a sign of vivacity; moderation; games that reveal character and sharpen wits) and I.3.13–17 (against corporal punishment: its three stated reasons, the problem of the older student, and the abuses done under cover of discipline). Exact-phrase search placed "a punishment fit for slaves", "imagine the age changed" and "I do not disapprove in boys, for it is a sign of vivacity" on the Honeycutt online edition of Watson's text, so those are Watson's phrases. I could not confirm the surrounding clauses I recall ("hardened, like the worst of slaves, even to stripes"; "not the least need of any such chastisement") and the registered scan was unreachable from this session, so the whole passage is paraphrased rather than quoted. The last sentences compress I.3.15–17, where Quintilian alludes to abuse of pupils by those permitted to beat them; Hearth's wording keeps it to what a parent needs. Section numbers are the conventional references; no page numbers claimed.
