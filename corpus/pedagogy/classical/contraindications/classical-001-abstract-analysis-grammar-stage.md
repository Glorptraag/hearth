## Reasoning

The classical tradition orders learning. Comenius's rule that nature makes no leaps and begins with the whole before the parts, and Quintilian's insistence that a young child's work be proportioned to their strength and never allowed to curdle into dread, together produce the tradition's most practical warning for a homeschooling parent: do not ask a six- or seven-year-old to do the work of the logic stage. Asking a grammar-stage child why the author chose that word, what the theme of the story is, how two civilisations compare, whether a character was right, or to write a paragraph defending an opinion asks for a tool the child has not yet been given — the facts and words are not yet stored, and the habit of reasoning from them has not yet formed. The tradition observes two outcomes, both bad. Either the child fails at the task and learns, as Quintilian feared, to dread the subject before they are old enough to love it; or, more insidiously, the child succeeds by handing back the adult's own analysis in the adult's own words. The second outcome looks like precocity and is in fact mimicry — Quintilian's own caveat about imitation that copies the person rather than the lesson — and it fakes the logic stage so convincingly that the real one may never get built. What the tradition asks of the grammar-stage child instead is plenty: collect, name, chant, copy the good model exactly, retell the fable in plain words, meet the thing itself through the senses, and store what is worth storing. The analysis comes, and comes eagerly, when the child begins arguing of their own accord.

Tension with other traditions: Unschooling sequences nothing by stage; the child's own question sets the order, so when a six-year-old asks why the villain did it, the unschooling parent follows that question wherever it leads and would read the classical restraint as withholding. The classical answer is that it withholds nothing from the conversation, only the demand — a child's spontaneous "why" is always welcome; what the tradition declines to do is require analysis before the tools for it exist. Charlotte Mason's tradition objects from the other side: Mason held that even the youngest child's mind feeds on ideas rather than bare facts, and her inheritors read any "fill the memory first, think later" sequence as a diminishment of a child who is already a whole person. The classical reply is that a memorised poem is an idea stored whole, not a bare fact, and that its meaning opening years later is the tradition working as designed. Both objections have coherent internal reasoning; an Eclectic family here is choosing between theories of when a child's reasoning should be asked for, not splitting the difference.
