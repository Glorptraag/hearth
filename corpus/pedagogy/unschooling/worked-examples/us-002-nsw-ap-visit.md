## Scenario

Mia (12) is being unschooled in NSW. The annual Authorised Person home visit is in two weeks. The mother has been keeping informal notes but feels her documentation is "thin" and is panicking. She's logging an entry asking for help putting together a learning record that will satisfy the AP without misrepresenting how Mia actually spends her days.

## Interpretation

The mother's panic is the right thing to address first, because panicked documentation reads as panicked documentation and that is what makes AP visits go badly. The good news is that NSW has been registering unschooling families successfully for decades — practitioners with much "thinner" documentation than the mother thinks she has have walked through this many times.

Operationally, the next two weeks should look like this:

Week one: gather what already exists. Phone photos, sketchbook contents, library checkout history, screen time spent on games and websites the mother can identify, books read aloud, conversations the mother remembers, places visited. This is almost always more than the mother thinks. Spread it across the kitchen table physically. Most mothers in this situation discover they have an entire term of evidence, just not in the form they were imagining.

Week one, second pass: translate into curriculum-area language. Take each piece of evidence and ask which Australian Curriculum learning area or NESA syllabus outcome it speaks to. A Minecraft city is design and digital technologies plus mathematics plus literacy if Mia is reading instructions plus arguably civics if she is moderating a server. A library run with three novels is English. A morning at the museum bug exhibit is science. A long conversation about why grandma is sad is health and personal development. The translation is not invention — it is the same events, named in the framework's vocabulary.

Week two: write the learning record as a brief narrative organised by learning area, not by date. The AP wants to see that learning is happening across the breadth of the curriculum. A two-page document with three to five examples per learning area, each example specific and dated, is dramatically more compelling than a daily log. Quote Mia where possible. Include photos.

Week two, the day before the visit: brief Mia gently and once. "An adult is coming tomorrow to chat with us about how we learn. You don't need to do anything special — just be yourself. If she asks you what you've been doing, tell her honestly." That is the entire prep. No coaching, no rehearsal, no anxiety projected onto Mia. The AP wants to meet a real twelve-year-old, not a performance.

During the visit: the mother should be calm, the documentation should be on the table, and the conversation should be allowed to happen. APs are people, not auditors. They are looking for evidence that the child is learning and being treated well, both of which are true.

The practitioner who has done this thirty-seven times across her own children and in coaching others is Sue Elvis. Her published material on NSW visits is the most useful single resource on this specific topic. Hearth families in NSW should be pointed to it directly.
