## Scenario

"Tahlia (10) finally recited the whole of 'Clancy of the Overflow' for Nan on FaceTime tonight — all eight verses, word-perfect, after three weeks of two lines a day at breakfast. Nan cried. Then, about ten seconds after she hung up, Tahlia said, 'But Mum, the man in the poem has never even been to the Overflow. He says himself it's his fancy. He's just imagining Clancy's happy. Clancy could be miserable out there with the flies.' I said that was sort of the point, and she said no it wasn't, the poet was being unfair to the city, and we argued about it for a solid twenty minutes while I was trying to do the dishes. She would not let it go. I'm logging the recitation, but I honestly don't know whether the argument was the lesson going off the rails or the best bit."

## Interpretation

It was the best bit, and it was also the lesson working exactly as the tradition says it should. Three weeks of two lines a day is the grammar stage done properly — small steps, no leaps, each day's lines laid on yesterday's and returned to — and the proof of it is a ten-year-old who can hold eight verses in her head and deliver them to her grandmother. Quintilian would want that recitation written down and counted: memory that receives with ease and retains with fidelity is the first evidence of a learning mind, and the poem is now furniture Tahlia will live with for the rest of her life. Nan crying is not incidental either. Recitation has always been a thing done for someone.

What happened ten seconds later is the next stage announcing itself. Tahlia did not argue that the poem was boring; she argued that its speaker had no grounds for his claim — that he was supposing Clancy's contentment, not knowing it — and she found the line in the poem where he admits as much. That is a logic-stage observation, and a sharp one. She noticed the gap between what the speaker knows and what he asserts, which is exactly the kind of inconsistency the tradition watches for as the sign that a child is ready to be taught to reason and not only to remember. The twenty minutes at the sink was a disputation, conducted without anyone naming it.

The classical tutor's move from here is to let it become one on purpose. Next time it comes up — and it will — the tradition would have her put her case in a single sentence ("the poet doesn't know Clancy is happy, he's guessing"), then go to the lines and sort what the speaker actually says he knows from what he supposes; then take the other side herself and argue that the poem is about the dreaming, not about Clancy at all. She already has the words by heart, so she can quote them, which is what the memorising was for. The one thing the tradition would not do is ask her to write an essay about it yet. Arguing it aloud is the right work for ten; saying it well on paper is the stage after this one, and there is no hurry.
