## Reasoning

The sharpest critique of unschooling comes from inside it. Practitioners who have been at this for decades say, in different words, the same thing: the most damaging misuse of the word is by a parent who has stopped doing anything and calls the stopping trust. Sandra Dodd has written for years against treating unschooling as "doing nothing", and the community's standing joke — that radical unschooling is more work than school, not less — is a joke because it is true. The tradition removes the curriculum. It does not remove the parent.

What trust consists of, in this tradition, is a set of active behaviours that happen to be quiet. Noticing — knowing what the child is into this week, what they are stuck on, what they said at dinner. Strewing — placing interesting things in the path, which requires knowing the child well enough to guess what might land. Conversation — being available as the person the child thinks aloud with. Connecting — finding the book, the person, the place, the club. Driving. Saying yes. And for Australian families, documentation: the retrospective noticing-in-writing that the Home Education Unit or NESA will ask for, and which is also, not coincidentally, how a parent discovers whether they have been paying attention. Holt's trust was earned through years of the closest observation of children anyone had done; it was never a licence to look away. A parent who cannot say what their child did yesterday is not trusting the child. They are not watching.

How practitioners tell the two apart. The honest tests the community uses are behavioural, not philosophical. Does the parent know what the child is currently absorbed in, in detail? Is the house changing — new things appearing, old things used in new ways — or has it been the same for months? Does the child bring things to the parent, or has the child learned the parent is not really available? When the child asks for something — a book, a lift, a lesson, a person — does it happen? Is the parent's own life visibly interesting, or has the parent also stopped? Is there any record at all — photos, a notebook, a Logger — or does the year exist only as a general impression? Deschooling has a legitimate "doing nothing" phase, and this corpus describes it; the difference is that a deschooling parent is present through it, watching it, and can describe it. Neglect cannot describe itself.

What the evidence says, in Hearth's words. In Gray and Riley's 2015 survey of 75 grown unschoolers (CC BY), the respondents who looked back unhappily were few, and what they described was not freedom but absence — homes marked by parental depression and disengagement, where the children were left to themselves rather than trusted. The adults who flourished described engaged parents who were around, interested and willing to help. The data cannot separate the method from the parent; the tradition's own reading is that this is because, in unschooling, the parent is the method.

Counter-position within the movement: some practitioners resist this framing because the accusation of laziness is exactly what outsiders throw at every unschooling family, including the engaged ones, and because a parent in a hard season — illness, grief, a new baby — can look disengaged for months and still be doing enough. Both points are fair. The warning here is not against hard seasons. It is against the permanent state, and against using the tradition's language to avoid noticing that the state has become permanent.

Honest status: this is the contraindication most likely to be true of a family and least likely to be logged by them. It shows up indirectly — in a Logger that has gone quiet, in entries that say "free day" without a single concrete noun, in a parent who can describe their philosophy fluently and their child's week not at all. Hearth should treat the absence of specifics over time as information, and should say so gently, because the parent in this position usually already suspects it.

Tension with other traditions: Charlotte Mason's "masterly inactivity" is sometimes cited as the respectable version of what this entry warns against, and the comparison is worth making because the two traditions agree. Masterly inactivity is active, watchful restraint — the parent present and attentive and choosing not to intervene — and Mason's own account of it is a matter of authority and attention, not indifference. Unschooling's trust is the same posture under a different name: the restraint is visible only against a background of attention. Both traditions would say that a parent who is absent is practising neither method, and an Eclectic family should hear that this is one of the places where two quite different philosophies arrive at the same warning.
