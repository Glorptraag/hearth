## Text

Between the change of teeth and puberty, Steiner says, the forces that were building the body are set free, and the educator may now work on them directly — on habit, character, conscience and memory. The means suited to this age are not explanations but living pictures and living authority. The child of seven to fourteen needs people to look up to: adults whose word is taken as true and good not because it has been argued for but because of who they are. Steiner regards this reverence as a healthy need of the age rather than a weakness to be trained out, and as the very ground on which later independent judgement will stand. Teaching should come as pictures, stories and parables that speak to the feelings — and the teacher must believe in the picture. His own example is the caterpillar and the butterfly as an image of the soul and the body; offered as a clever comparison the teacher does not hold to, it does nothing, but offered as something the teacher sees as true, it works deeply.

This is also the age of memory. Much should be learned by heart — verses, tables, stories — that will be understood only later, because understanding that follows remembering is healthy, while understanding forced before its time dries up the child's forces. Abstract concepts and the demand that the child form independent judgements belong to the years after puberty, when the capacity for them is born; to press them on the younger child, Steiner says, is to impoverish the whole of their later life.
