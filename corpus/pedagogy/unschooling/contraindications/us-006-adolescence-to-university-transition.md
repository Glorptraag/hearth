## Reasoning

The unschooling tradition's honest position on adolescence is that something does change — not the trust, but the horizon. A nine-year-old's learning has no deadline. A sixteen-year-old who wants to study nursing, or get an apprenticeship, or sit in a university lecture theatre, has acquired one, and the tradition's own practitioners have long said that the late-teen years are where self-direction either becomes real or is revealed to have been the parent's doctrine rather than the young person's practice. The warning is not against unschooling teenagers. It is against families who treat "we never impose structure" as a rule that overrides the young person's own stated goal, when the honest unschooling response to a goal is to help the young person build whatever structure the goal requires.

What the evidence says, in Hearth's words. Gray and Riley's second report on 75 grown unschoolers (2015, CC BY) found that most of those who went on to university got in without serious difficulty, and typically by routes that did not require a school record: starting at a community college and transferring (the Australian equivalents are a TAFE certificate or diploma, or a university enabling programme), sitting an admissions test, or being admitted on interview and a portfolio of their own work. Once there, the adjustment most of them described was not academic failure but friction with the form — deadlines, examinations, lectures, and most often classmates who seemed to have no interest in learning. A minority named gaps, maths most commonly, that they closed deliberately once they had a reason to. The employment picture was strong: a large majority were in work that grew directly out of childhood interests, around half were self-employed, and creative and technical fields were heavily represented. The authors are plain that the sample was self-selected through Gray's own readership and has no comparison group, so these are the experiences of people who chose to report them, not a population estimate. Hearth families should know the findings are encouraging and should know their limits.

What the movement itself says about preparation. Practitioners who have raised unschoolers into adulthood describe the late teens as a period of self-directed catch-up: the young person works out what the door they want actually requires, and then does that work — often fast, often with a tutor, a TAFE course or a term of structured maths — because it is now theirs. Grace Llewellyn's writing for unschooled teenagers, Blake Boles's on alternatives to the standard route, and the community guidance circulating since the 1990s all land in the same place: the preparation is real, it is chosen, and it is late only by school's calendar. The parent's job shifts too — from strewing and conversation toward brokering: finding out what the admissions pathway actually is this year, driving to the open day, paying for the bridging unit, helping assemble a portfolio from the years of real work that already exist.

The Australian specifics are more forgiving than most parents fear. An ATAR is a school product and home-educated young people mostly do not have one; universities admit without it through TAFE articulation, enabling and preparation programmes, Open Universities Australia units taken as a demonstration of capability, the STAT for applicants without a school result, and at some institutions portfolio or early-entry schemes. Eligibility shifts year to year and institution to institution, so the current admissions page, not a forum post, is the source. TAFE will often enrol a sixteen-year-old directly into a certificate. In Queensland, HEU registration and annual reporting continue through the senior years, and a programme for a sixteen-year-old that reads like the plan for a nine-year-old signals to the reviewer what it ought to signal to the parent.

Counter-position within the movement: some practitioners hold that even this framing concedes too much — that university is one option among many, that anxiety about "preparation" is school-shaped, and that a young person who genuinely wants a degree will find their way to it without the parent's planning. There is truth in this; the grown unschoolers in the research largely did. The honest reply is that they did so because an adult helped them find the door when they asked, and the warning here is aimed at the household where no one is answering.

Honest status: the tradition's failure mode in adolescence is not too little structure imposed by the parent. It is a young person with a goal and a parent who, out of loyalty to a principle, declines to help build the scaffolding the goal needs. "I never make them do anything" is an answer about the parent; the question was about the young person. Families who treat "no structure ever" as doctrine should recognise that they have turned a method of trust into a rule, which is the thing the method was meant to dissolve.

Tension with other traditions: Classical education and Charlotte Mason's upper forms both assume that structure increases through adolescence and is set by the adult — the rhetoric stage, the examination syllabus. Unschooling disagrees about who sets it, not about whether a sixteen-year-old with a goal might sit a structured maths course. An Eclectic family blending unschooling with a Classical senior plan should see that the two traditions give coherent but different answers to "whose plan is this?", and choose one rather than averaging them.
