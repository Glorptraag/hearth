## Scenario

"Arlo (6). We spent the morning in the vegie patch getting the beds ready for the winter brassicas — I was turning the compost and barrowing mulch, he was mostly 'helping' with his little wooden barrow and then wandered off to the trampoline. After lunch he was out there on his own for well over an hour being 'the farmer.' He loaded the barrow with gum leaves, pushed it to the back fence, tipped it, came back, loaded it again. He had my old hat on and I heard him say to nobody, 'Right, one more load and then smoko.' When I went out to join in he said, 'No, Mum, I'm working.' Nanna rang while he was at it and asked what he'd learnt today and I said 'he was a farmer for an hour' and she laughed. I'm not sure what to write here. Is pushing a barrow around for an hour learning?"

## Interpretation

Yes — and it is the kind of learning this tradition would most want to see at six. The morning went into Arlo through his eyes and his limbs, and it came back out in the afternoon as his own deed. That is imitation doing exactly what Steiner says it does before the change of teeth: the child does not learn the garden by being told about compost, he becomes someone who gardens by copying the gardener. Notice that he copied more than the task. "One more load and then smoko" is your rhythm and your words, and the hat is your hat. The adult's inner attitude is the real curriculum in these years, and Arlo has shown you what yours looks like from the outside — unhurried, a bit wry, work followed by a rest. That is worth a quiet moment of pride.

The hour itself matters too. Load, push, tip, return, again, with the whole body and no product at the end — that is will-activity, and the tradition holds that the will is strengthened by exactly this kind of absorbed, repeated doing long before the intellect is asked for anything. You did right not to join when he said no. Had you gone out and counted the loads with him, or explained what the mulch does for the soil, you would have pulled him out of the picture and into your head, and the tradition would rather he stayed in his own.

For the Logger, write it as it was. In this tradition's terms it is evidence of a healthy six-year-old: rhythmic real work in the morning, free imitative play after it, body fully used, the home being absorbed. When the Home Education Unit asks, that is physical development, responsibility, and a child taking up his place in the working world of the family — all true, and all visible in one afternoon. As for Nanna, the picture of the farmer is the lesson; it does not need translating into something that sounds more like school. Tomorrow the thing to do is keep the rhythm: work in the garden in the morning, free play after, the hat back on its hook where he can reach it.
