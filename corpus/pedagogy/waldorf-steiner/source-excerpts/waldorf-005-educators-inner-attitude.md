## Text

Opening the course with which the first Waldorf school was founded, Steiner told the teachers that their task was, before anything else, an inner one. What reaches the child most strongly is not the method or the material but the attitude the adult carries into the room — the reverence or the impatience, the real interest or the indifference, the thoughts the teacher thinks about a particular child. He treats these unspoken things as the true means of education, working on the child through what he calls the imponderables, the influences that pass between people without being said. He therefore asked the teachers to take their own self-education seriously as the first part of the work, and to meet each child as a being who has arrived with something of their own to unfold, which the educator serves rather than manufactures. In his picture, education continues work that began before the child was born, and deserves the same reverence as anything placed into one's hands in trust.
