## What it indicates

A child who does the same exercise over and over, with no one asking and nothing promised, and then stops of his own accord, is showing Montessori's strongest single piece of evidence that an exercise has met a developmental need. She counted. Children of three to three and a half repeated the cylinders "up to forty times without losing their interest in it"; some tire after five or six, others go to twenty; a four-year-old went on past her sixteenth round while Montessori had the other children sing to try to distract her. The repetition is not stubbornness or "obsession". "The exercise which develops life, consists in the repetition, not in the mere grasp of the idea", and "repetition corresponds to a need". So the repeating is both the proof that the material is right and the developmental event itself.

The stopping matters as much as the repeating. The end of a true cycle comes from inside: the child puts the material away, looks up, and wanders off, satisfied and calm. Montessori notes that "The majority of our children become calm as they go through such exercises"; the settled face afterwards is part of the marker. What the tradition does not count is repetition held in place by an adult's attention (a child who glances up for approval between rounds), or repetition that looks anxious and leaves the child wound tighter rather than calmer.

Absence is information too. A child who handles a material once and leaves it, week after week, is telling the environment that this material has come too early or too late; the tradition reads that as a mismatch of moment, not a failing in the child.

## Look for

- The same exercise done many times in a row — five, twenty, forty — without anyone suggesting it or counting aloud
- Absorption that survives ordinary distraction: a sibling singing, someone walking past, the kettle
- A clear ending the child chooses: he returns the material to its place, stretches, looks up, goes to find a snack
- A calm, settled face afterwards rather than agitation or a demand for the next thing
- No glance toward the adult for approval during or after the rounds
- Over weeks, the repeated exercise dropped and a harder one taken up on his own — the need has been met
- The reverse signal: a material tried once and abandoned, repeatedly, which points to a mismatch of moment rather than to the child
