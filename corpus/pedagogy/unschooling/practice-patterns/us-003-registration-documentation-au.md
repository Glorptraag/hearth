## Trigger

Australian unschooling parent is preparing materials for HEU (QLD), NESA (NSW), or VRQA (VIC) registration or annual report. They are anxious because their child's learning does not look like school, the registration paperwork asks for things that look like school, and they don't know how to bridge the gap honestly.

## Response

This is the single most consequential operational skill an Australian unschooling parent develops. The good news: it is a learnable skill and many families have done it successfully across all state jurisdictions.

The core move is what Sue Elvis calls translation — taking what actually happened (a child built a Minecraft city; a child cared for a sick chicken; a child read four chapters of a Roald Dahl book to their younger sibling; a family went to the museum and the child spent forty minutes at the bug exhibit) and rendering it in language the registration body recognises (computational thinking and spatial design; biology and animal care; reading fluency and prosocial behaviour; informal science learning and sustained attention). The events are real. The translation is the skill.

A few principles make this easier:

- Document continuously rather than retrospectively. A short note in the moment ("Liam spent the morning with the chickens, noticed one was lethargic, asked questions about why, looked up symptoms with me on the phone") is much easier to translate than a memory three months old.
- Use the curriculum framework's own language without lying about what happened. Australian Curriculum learning area descriptors are general enough that authentic learning almost always maps to several of them. The translation is not invention — it is selection.
- Photograph and screenshot. A photo of the child's drawing, the page they read, the screen they were on, the place they visited. These become evidence without requiring narrative construction.
- Curate, don't fabricate. Pick the strongest examples from a term, not every moment. A registration body wants to see learning happened — it does not need an exhaustive log.
- Lead with the child's voice where possible. Quoting the child ("Mum, why do bees only sting once?") is often more compelling than parental description and is unambiguously authentic.
- In QLD specifically: the HEU has reviewed and modernised processes since 2024. The Queensland Home Education Unit accepts narrative-based reporting and has a track record of accepting unschooling-style learning plans when they are clearly articulated in curriculum terms.
- In NSW specifically: the Authorised Person home visit is the major variable. Documentation should be ready to walk through verbally, not just on paper. Sue Elvis has 27 years of successful NSW visits and has written extensively on what to expect.

The deeper point: the registration system is the parent's problem to solve, not the child's. The child should not be coached, prepped, or asked to perform for the AP visit. The child should continue their day. The parent absorbs the regulatory burden so the unschooling can remain authentic.

## Anti-pattern

Do not invent learning that did not happen. Do not retroactively add "learning objectives" the child was not pursuing. Do not coach the child to recite things for the AP. Do not let the registration cycle drive the home — the cycle should be a quarterly inconvenience, not a year-round shadow curriculum.
