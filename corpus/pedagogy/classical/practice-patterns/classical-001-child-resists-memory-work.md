## Trigger

The parent has set a piece of memory work — a poem, the seven-times table, the list of Australian prime ministers, a Latin declension, a Bible verse, a speech from a play — and the child groans, stalls, says it is boring or pointless, gets three lines in and stops, or recites yesterday's lines in a flat sing-song while looking out the window. The parent is starting to wonder whether this child "just isn't a memoriser" and whether to drop it.

## Response

The classical tradition does not read this as a reason to drop memory work. Quintilian names memory as the first sign of a learning mind and says childhood is when it is most retentive; Comenius builds his whole method on filling the early years with what the later years will reason about. A child who resists memorising is still a child whose memory is at its sharpest, and the tradition would rather fix the drill than lose the decade.

It reads the resistance, instead, as information about the drill, and checks three things in order. First, the form. Quintilian is emphatic that a young child's learning must be a pleasure and that the will cannot be compelled, and the classical home has always met this with chant, song, rhythm and movement. A times table marched around the backyard one number per step, a poem sung to a tune the child already knows, a list turned into call-and-response with the parent taking the odd lines and the child the even ones, a verse recited to the dog or to Nan on the phone — these are not softenings of memory work, they are how memory work has been done for two thousand years. A child who will not sit and chant will very often skip and chant. Second, the size. Comenius's rule that nature makes no leaps applies to a poem as much as to arithmetic: two new lines a day, laid on yesterday's and reviewed, arrive at a whole poem in a month without a groan, where eight lines demanded at once produce a groan on day one. If the child stalls at line four, the tradition's reading is that line three is not yet secure, not that the child cannot learn. Third, what is being memorised. Quintilian wanted the sentences a child copies and the passages a child learns by heart to be worth having for life — the best poets, sayings that will still be true at sixty. A child asked to memorise filler is making a fair judgement about the filler.

Two distinctions the tradition would want the parent to hold while they do this. A child does not need to understand what they memorise for the memorising to be worth doing — a seven-year-old can carry a stanza for years before its meaning opens, and the tradition regards that as the point rather than a flaw. And a word-perfect recitation is not understanding either; it is the grammar stage's work done well, and the questions about meaning belong to the stage after it. In an older child — ten, eleven, twelve — resistance to memory work is sometimes not about the drill at all but a demand to know why, and the tradition reads that as the logic stage announcing itself. The classical reply then is not more chanting but an argument: the child makes the case against memorising this, and the parent makes the case for it back, as an opponent rather than a judge.

## Anti-pattern

The tradition's own first teacher ruled out the obvious wrong moves. Not punishment, and not the threat of it — Quintilian held that a child driven by fear simply hardens, and that nothing is left to drive them with once they are older. Not adding lines as a consequence of resistance, since the size was the likely problem. Not dropping memory work altogether on the theory that this child's mind is not the memorising kind — the tradition does not believe such children are common. And not demanding an explanation of the meaning as a condition of having "really" learned it, which asks the grammar stage to do the logic stage's job and spoils both.
