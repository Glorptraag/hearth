## Scenario

Eli (9) has been "doing nothing" for three weeks. The family deschooled out of conventional school four months ago. He spent the first month happy and exploring, the second month bored and complaining, and the third month playing the same video game for hours every day. The mother is starting to feel like she made a mistake leaving school. She's logging this entry with worry.

## Interpretation

This is the deschooling progression, exactly as the practitioners describe it. The first month of celebration, the second month of boredom, and now the third month — and what looks like "the same video game for hours every day" is, in this tradition, almost certainly Eli doing real work. The mother's instinct to read it as failure is the school-conditioned anxiety doing its job.

A few specific things would help here. First, the mother should sit beside Eli for an hour and ask him about the game — not testing him, just genuinely curious. Most parents who do this come away surprised at how much is actually happening: planning, problem-solving, reading menus and instructions, often communication with other players, often substantial mathematics in resource management. The game is not "nothing." Naming what is in it is the first step out of the false framing.

Second, the mother should look at the rest of life and ask whether it is genuinely interesting and available right now. Not "is it educational" — is it interesting? A trip to a place worth visiting, a meal with people worth eating with, a half-built thing in the kitchen the child can join. Not as covert intervention to lure Eli off the screen, but because if the rest of life is thin, the screen is the only place anything is happening, and that is the parent's territory to fix.

Third, and most importantly: the mother should hold her nerve. Four months in is early. The Gray and Riley research, the practitioner consensus, the testimonies of grown unschoolers — all of it points to this period being normal and not predictive. Children in deschooling do not progress on a tidy curve. The mother who panics here and re-imposes structure typically resets the whole clock. The mother who holds steady through the third and fourth months almost always sees a fifth month that looks materially different.

The most useful specific thing the mother could do this week: stop logging "Eli did nothing today" in her own head, and start logging "Eli played [specific game] for [duration], appeared [emotional state], we [whatever shared time happened]." The shift in framing is half the work.
