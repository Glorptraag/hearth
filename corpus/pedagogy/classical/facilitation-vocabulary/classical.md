## Verbs

- **Memorise** — Put words worth keeping into the child's memory while memory is at its most tenacious. Quintilian held that memory is the chief sign of ability in a child, so the classical home treats a poem, a psalm, a times table, a list of rivers or kings as furniture the mind will live with for life. Small daily portions, said aloud, added to and returned to — never crammed.
- **Recite** — Say the memorised thing aloud, whole, to someone. Recitation is the grammar stage's performance and its check: the words either come or they don't. A recitation to Nan on the phone counts for as much as one at the kitchen table.
- **Imitate** — Copy a good model exactly before making anything of one's own. Quintilian put imitation second only to memory as the sign of a teachable child, and made copying fine sentences the beginning of writing. The parent's job is to choose a model worth copying — a real poet's stanza, a clean working, a well-made paragraph — and let the child reproduce its shape, not just its words.
- **Retell** — Tell a fable or story back in the child's own plain words, then tell it again another way. This is Quintilian's first composition exercise (Aesop retold simply, then written out), and Erasmus's copia — abundance of expression — is its grown-up form: say the same thing six ways until the words are the child's servants rather than their masters.
- **Order** — Arrange what is to be learned from easier to harder, from the whole to its parts, one step at a time. Comenius's rule that nature makes no leaps is the classical tutor's planning principle: today's lesson should rest on yesterday's and reach only as far as tomorrow's can follow.
- **Define** — Ask what a word means before arguing about it. Half the logic stage is discovering that an argument has stalled because two people mean different things by "fair" or "alive" or "a country". A definition the child can state and defend is a tool, not a vocabulary exercise.
- **Question** — Ask rather than tell, in the Socratic manner: a question that makes the child examine what they already believe, followed by another that tests the answer. The parent withholds the conclusion on purpose. The aim is not to trap the child but to let them watch their own reasoning work.
- **Dispute** — Give the child a position to defend and a worthy objection to answer. Disputation was the medieval classroom's game and it is the logic stage's natural outlet: state the case, hear the strongest thing against it, reply. Then swap sides. Arguing with a parent who is playing the opponent on purpose is a different thing from arguing with a parent who wants to win.

## Restraints

- **Do not ask a grammar-stage child for abstract analysis** — A six-year-old can collect, name, chant, copy and retell. Asking them why the author chose that word, or to compare two civilisations, asks for a tool they have not yet been handed. They will either fail and learn to dread it, or hand back the adult's own opinion dressed up as reasoning.
- **Do not rush to rhetoric before the tools are in hand** — An opinion with no facts and no line of reasoning under it is not self-expression; it is noise the child will later have to unlearn. The tradition is patient about letting a child "have a view" until the child can also say what they know and how they got there.
- **Do not mistake memorisation for understanding** — A word-perfect recitation proves the memory did its work. It does not prove the child grasps what the words mean, and the classical tutor does not pretend it does. Understanding is the next stage's business and arrives on its own timetable.
- **Do not mistake understanding for the ability to express it** — A child who plainly grasps a thing may still be unable to say it well. That gap is exactly what the rhetoric stage exists to close, and it is not a failure of understanding. Judge the two separately.
- **Do not force memory work with pressure or punishment** — Quintilian rejected the rod outright and insisted the will cannot be compelled to learn. Resistance to a chant is a message about the form of the chant — make it a song, a march, a race, a game — or about its size. It is not a reason to add consequences or to add lines.
- **Do not skip steps** — Comenius's nature makes no leaps. If the child cannot do today's step, the fault is almost always in the step before it, not in the child. Go back one and secure the foundation before putting up the wall.
- **Do not give the child a poor model** — Imitation only works if what is imitated is excellent. A weak model copied carefully is still a weak model learned. Choose the real poem, the real proof, the real paragraph.
- **Do not answer your own question** — A Socratic question followed by the parent's own answer teaches the child to wait you out. Ask, and then bear the silence until they have thought.
- **Do not win the disputation** — The parent playing opponent is there to make the child's argument stronger, not to defeat it. A child who loses every argument to a cleverer adult stops arguing, and the logic stage closes before it has properly opened.

## Micro-scripts

- **Starting a new piece of memory work** — Two lines today. Say them with me three times, then go and say them to the dog.
- **When the child groans at a chant** — Let's walk it instead — one step for every word, right around the backyard. Or I'll say the odd ones and you say the evens, and we'll see who gets stuck first.
- **When the child interrupts a recitation to ask why** — Hold that thought. Get to the end of the verse, and then we'll sit down and argue about it properly.
- **When the child gives an opinion with nothing under it** — Maybe. What do you know that makes you think so? Give me two things.
- **When the child spots an inconsistency or starts an argument** — Good catch. Put your case in one sentence. Then I'm going to give you the strongest thing against it, and you have to answer that.
- **When setting copywork or imitation** — Copy it exactly as the poet wrote it, every comma. Then write one of your own in the same shape about something you saw today.
- **When the child wants to be told the answer** — I'm not going to tell you this one. What would have to be true for your idea to work? Start there.
