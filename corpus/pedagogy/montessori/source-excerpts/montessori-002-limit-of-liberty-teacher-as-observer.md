## Text

The liberty of the child should have as its *limit* the collective interest; as its *form*, what we universally consider good breeding. We must, therefore, check in the child whatever offends or annoys others, or whatever tends toward rough or ill-bred acts. But all the rest,—every manifestation having a useful scope,—whatever it be, and under whatever form it expresses itself, must not only be permitted, but must be *observed* by the teacher. Here lies the essential point; from her scientific preparation, the teacher must bring not only the capacity, but the desire, to observe natural phenomena. In our system, she must become a passive, much more than an active, influence, and her passivity shall be composed of anxious scientific curiosity, and of absolute *respect* for the phenomenon which she wishes to observe. The teacher must understand and *feel* her position of *observer*: the *activity* must lie in the *phenomenon*.

[…]

It is of course understood, that here we do not speak of useless or dangerous acts, for these must be *suppressed*, *destroyed*.

## Context

Verbatim from the George translation, checked against the PG #39863 plain text (GITenberg mirror). Two blocks from the same stretch of Chapter V; the […] marks one omitted paragraph (on respecting the child's first spontaneous manifestations and avoiding "the arrest of spontaneous movements and the imposition of arbitrary tasks"), and the final sentence is the one that closes that omitted paragraph. Italics and dashes normalised as elsewhere. Confidence in wording: high. "Anxious" here carries its older sense of eager, not worried.

Why it is load-bearing: this is where Montessori draws the one line her liberty does not cross. Everything "having a useful scope" is permitted and watched; what is rough, ill-bred, useless or dangerous is checked. It is the direct ground for the material-misuse pattern and for the facilitation vocabulary's "observe from distance".
